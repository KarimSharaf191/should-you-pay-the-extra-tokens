\begin{abstract}
Checking whether a claim is supported by a source document is a basic step
in evaluating LLM outputs in high-stakes, document-heavy settings. When the
document is long, the check itself becomes expensive. Small LLMs (under 10B
parameters) are cheap enough to run it at scale, and inference-time
scaffolds such as chain-of-thought prompting, native reasoning and
retrieval can make them more accurate, but they also use more tokens. We
measure accuracy and token use together for six scaffolds, Qwen3 models
from 1.7B to 8B with thinking on and off, and three benchmarks
(ContractNLI, FinDVer and CoverBench). At 8B, native reasoning gives the
largest gains, up to $+0.18$ balanced accuracy, and chain-of-thought with
thinking is the most accurate condition on average. The value of the other
scaffolds depends on the benchmark: retrieval keeps accuracy on
ContractNLI with 4.8$\times$ fewer input tokens, while a recursive language
model reads ten times more tokens than the other scaffolds without being
more accurate. Below 8B, the cheapest good scaffold changes with the
benchmark. Finally, the model relies on the gold evidence when it has it,
but agentic retrieval often answers before it has found any of it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Many tasks come down to deciding whether a claim is supported by a
document: contract review, fact-checking, and checking the output of
language models \citep{jacovi2024coverbench}. This is harder when the
document is long, such as a full contract or a quarterly report. The
evidence may sit in one clause, be spread over several sections, or only
follow from combining text and tables
\citep{koreeda-manning-2021-contractnli, zhao-etal-2024-findver}.

LLMs can now read hundreds of thousands of tokens
\citep{gao2025longcontext, yang2025qwen3}, and recent work tests them on
claim verification over long documents \citep{karpinska2024nocha,
zhao-etal-2024-findver, elaraby2025synclaimeval}. Small open models are
attractive for this because they can be self-hosted, but they still trail
larger models \citep{zhao-etal-2024-findver, karpinska2024nocha}.
Fine-tuning them on synthetic claims helps mainly on in-domain documents
\citep{tang2024minicheck, pham2025clipper, elaraby2025synclaimeval} and is
expensive for long inputs. A cheaper option is an \emph{inference-time
scaffold}: a procedure such as chain-of-thought prompting, retrieval or an
agent loop that spends more computation at test time without changing the
model.

Scaffolds are usually compared on accuracy alone. For a small,
self-hosted model, however, every extra token costs time and memory, and
scaffolds can differ in token use by an order of magnitude: retrieval adds
input tokens, reasoning adds output tokens, and repeated calls add both. A
scaffold that gains two points of accuracy with ten times the tokens may
or may not be worth it. We therefore measure accuracy and tokens together
and ask three questions:

\noindent \textbf{RQ1: Which scaffolds are worth their extra tokens?} Which
scaffolds give the best accuracy for their token cost, and do they add to
the model's native reasoning or replace it?

\noindent \textbf{RQ2: Does the answer change with model size?} Does the
ranking of scaffolds hold from 8B down to 1.7B, and how does the value of
native reasoning change?

\noindent \textbf{RQ3: Is the reasoning based on the evidence?} Do
predictions depend on the gold evidence, does agentic retrieval find it
before answering, and do chain-of-thought rationales quote it correctly?

We study Qwen3 \citep{yang2025qwen3} at 1.7B, 4B and 8B, each with thinking
on and off, on ContractNLI \citep{koreeda-manning-2021-contractnli}, FinDVer
\citep{zhao-etal-2024-findver} and CoverBench \citep{jacovi2024coverbench},
with Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct \citep{grattafiori2024llama3} as a reference
model without a thinking mode (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). We compare six
scaffolds, from a plain zero-shot prompt to a recursive language model,
and run every condition at every model size. Our contributions are a
taxonomy of scaffolds by what they change (\S\ref{sec:taxonomy}), an
evaluation that reports accuracy together with input and generated
tokens (\S\ref{sec:scaffolds}--\S\ref{sec:setup}), and the following
findings:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item At 8B, native reasoning gives the largest gains: up to $+0.170$
balanced accuracy (BA) on ContractNLI and $+0.176$ on CoverBench.
\item Chain-of-thought prompting adds nothing on top of native reasoning on
ContractNLI, but it does on FinDVer and CoverBench; averaged over the three
benchmarks, chain-of-thought with thinking is the most accurate condition.
\item Retrieval improves accuracy on the long FinDVer reports at 8B; on
ContractNLI it keeps accuracy with 4.8$\times$ fewer input tokens. The
recursive language model reads 23K--40K input tokens per claim and is
never the best choice.
\item Below 8B, the cheapest good scaffold depends on the benchmark:
chain-of-thought without thinking on FinDVer and CoverBench, retrieval with
thinking on ContractNLI.
\item The model relies on the gold evidence when it has it, but agentic
retrieval answers without having seen any of it in 26\% of ContractNLI and
16\% of FinDVer cases.
\end{itemize}
Code, data and per-instance results are available at
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/should-you-pay-the-extra-tokens-66EC}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\noindent \textbf{Claim verification over long documents.}
FEVER \citep{thorne2018fever} made fact verification popular, with short
claims and sentence-level evidence from Wikipedia. Later benchmarks ask for
a verdict against a single long source: ContractNLI
\citep{koreeda-manning-2021-contractnli} uses contracts and a three-way
label set, FinDVer \citep{zhao-etal-2024-findver} uses financial reports
with many tables, and CoverBench \citep{jacovi2024coverbench} collects hard
claims from several domains. These benchmarks are normally used to rank
models by accuracy; we use them to rank scaffolds by accuracy per token.

\noindent \textbf{Test-time compute.}
Chain-of-thought prompting asks the model to reason before answering
\citep{wei2022cot}, and self-consistency combines several sampled
reasoning paths \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}. Reasoning models are
trained to produce long reasoning traces \citep{deepseekai2025r1}, and
test-time scaling controls their length \citep{muennighoff2025s1}. This
work shows that more test-time compute can raise accuracy, but it rarely
reports what that compute costs.

\noindent \textbf{Retrieval.}
Retrieval-augmented generation gives the model passages selected from a
store \citep{lewis2020rag}; for long documents it also shortens the input.
Self-RAG learns when to retrieve \citep{asai2024selfrag}, and agentic
retrieval alternates search and reasoning over several turns
\citep{trivedi2023ircot, singh2025agenticrag}. Both change the number of
input tokens, which our cost measure (Eq.~\ref{eq:cost}) counts.

\noindent \textbf{Agents and recursion.}
Tool-using agents mix reasoning with actions \citep{yao2023react}.
Recursive language models \citep{zhang2025rlm} go further and let the
model call itself on parts of a long input.

\noindent \textbf{Cost-aware evaluation.}
FrugalGPT \citep{chen2024frugalgpt} chains LLM APIs to match the best model
at lower cost, \citet{kapoor2025agents} argue for cost--accuracy Pareto
frontiers in agent evaluation, and the Holistic Agent Leaderboard
\citep{kapoor2026hal} applies this at scale. These works compare models or
agent systems. We instead hold the model fixed and compare scaffolds,
across model sizes and domains, counting input and generated tokens
separately.

\section{A Taxonomy of Inference-Time Scaffolds}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

We group scaffolds by what they change in a fixed model's verification
procedure (Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy}). \emph{Context construction} changes
what the model reads, for example by replacing the document with retrieved
passages. \emph{Problem structure} changes how the task is presented, for
example the prompt format or splitting a claim into sub-claims.
\emph{Inference computation} changes how much the model generates or how
many calls it makes, for example a chain-of-thought rationale, native
reasoning, or recursive calls. The groups overlap: agentic retrieval
changes both what the model reads and how many calls it makes. The
taxonomy organizes our comparisons; whether scaffolds from different
groups add up is a question we test in \S\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Scaffolds}
\label{sec:scaffolds}
\label{sec:baselines}
\label{sec:problem}

An instance is a claim $c$ and a document $D$. A verifier built on a fixed
model $M$ returns a label $\hat{y}$ from the benchmark's label set
(\S\ref{sec:benchmarks}). The simplest verifier makes one call:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}=\operatorname{verdict}\!\left(M(P_0(c,D);r)\right),
    \label{eq:verification}
\end{equation}
where $P_0$ builds a prompt from the claim, the document and the label set,
$r\in\{\mathrm{off},\mathrm{on}\}$ switches native reasoning, and
$\operatorname{verdict}$ reads the label from the response. A scaffold
wraps the same model and may change the prompt, call the model several
times, or retrieve passages from $D$. Since $M$ is fixed, differences in
accuracy and cost come from the scaffold. The prompts are given in
Appendix~\ref{sec:prompts}.

\subsection{Context Construction}
\label{sec:context-construction}

\noindent \textbf{\textsc{rag}} \citep{lewis2020rag} gives the model the
spans of the document most relevant to the claim instead of the full
document. On ContractNLI the spans are the sentences and list items that
the dataset marks; we embed them and the claim with MiniLM
\citep{wang2020minilm}\footnote{The \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} checkpoint
of Sentence-Transformers \citep{reimers2019sbert}.} and keep the eight
most similar spans. On FinDVer the spans are the report's paragraphs and
on CoverBench chunks of the claim's source document; we rank them by BM25
\citep{robertson2009bm25} and by similarity of Qwen3-Embedding-4B vectors
\citep{zhang2025qwen3embedding}, merge the two rankings with reciprocal
rank fusion \citep{cormack2009rrf}, and keep the top ten. On CoverBench the
\textsc{rag} prompt also asks the model to reason step by step before its
verdict (Appendix~\ref{sec:prompts}).
% CHECK: state how CoverBench documents are split into chunks.

\noindent \textbf{\textsc{agentic rag}} \citep{singh2025agenticrag} lets the
model search, as in ReAct \citep{yao2023react} and IRCoT
\citep{trivedi2023ircot}. At each turn the model either writes a search
query, which returns the five highest-ranked spans from the same retriever
as \textsc{rag}, or gives its answer. It may search at most four times.

\subsection{Problem Structure}
\label{sec:problem-structure}

\noindent \textbf{\textsc{raw}} is a short zero-shot prompt with the
claim, the full document and the label set, sent in one call. On
CoverBench it is the zero-shot prompt released with the benchmark
\citep{jacovi2024coverbench}, on FinDVer it adapts the benchmark's
direct-answer prompt \citep{zhao-etal-2024-findver}, and on ContractNLI we
wrote our own.

\noindent \textbf{\typed{}} gives the model the same information in a
template generated from a DSPy signature \citep{khattab2023dspy}, with
typed input fields and a verdict field restricted to the allowed labels,
used zero-shot without optimization. \textsc{raw} and \typed{} differ only
in prompt format. Claim decomposition also belongs to this group, but we
do not evaluate it.

\subsection{Inference Computation}
\label{sec:inference-computation}

\noindent \textbf{\textsc{cot}} uses DSPy's chain-of-thought module
\citep{khattab2023dspy}, which adds a reasoning field before the label, so
the model writes its reasoning as part of its answer
\citep{kojima2022zeroshot, wei2022cot}.

\noindent \textbf{Native reasoning} keeps the prompt and turns on Qwen3's
thinking mode \citep{yang2025qwen3}, so the model reasons in a separate
thinking block before it answers. Both kinds of reasoning count as
generated tokens. We cross the thinking switch with all five scaffolds
above (\textsc{raw}, \typed{}, \textsc{cot}, \textsc{rag}, \textsc{agentic
rag}), which gives ten conditions per model and benchmark.
Appendix~\ref{sec:prompts} shows the prompts and outputs for the four
combinations of \textsc{cot} and thinking; comparing \textsc{cot}\,(on)
with \textsc{raw}\,(on) tests whether prompted and native reasoning add up.

\noindent \textbf{\textsc{rlm}} \citep{zhang2025rlm} gives the model the
document as a variable in a Python REPL. The model reads parts of it with
code and can send sub-questions about selected parts to new model calls,
for up to ten iterations at recursion depth one. Each claim takes about
ten calls, so we run \textsc{rlm} only with Qwen3-8B and thinking off.

We use three claim-verification benchmarks over long documents

\textbf{ContractNLI} \citep{koreeda-manning-2021-contractnli} pairs

claim. \textbf{FinDVer} \citep{zhao-etal-2024-findver} checks claims
against quarterly reports (Form 10-Q), which are over twenty times longer
than the other documents and contain dozens of tables; we use testmini,
the only split with public labels. \textbf{CoverBench}
\citep{jacovi2024coverbench} collects difficult claims from nine datasets
in different domains and tests whether results hold across them.
ContractNLI and FinDVer mark the gold evidence for each claim, which we
use in RQ3. The label balance differs a lot: only 9\% of ContractNLI
claims are \textsc{contradicted}, while FinDVer is balanced. This is why we
report balanced accuracy (\S\ref{sec:metrics}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Models and Decoding}
\label{sec:models}

We use Qwen3 at 1.7B, 4B and 8B \citep{yang2025qwen3}, whose thinking mode
can be switched on or off in the same weights, and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct
\citep{grattafiori2024llama3} as a model without a thinking mode, run with
all five scaffolds. Qwen3-8B and Llama ran through the OpenRouter API.
Qwen3-4B and 1.7B ran locally, with vLLM \citep{kwon2023vllm} on a single
NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU for ContractNLI and with Hugging Face Transformers
\citep{wolf2020transformers} for FinDVer and CoverBench; the Qwen3-8B \textsc{rlm} and
RQ3 runs also used the local vLLM server, which gives almost the same
zero-shot ContractNLI result as the API (0.529 vs.\ 0.531 BA).
% CHECK: add the GPU used for the FinDVer and CoverBench runs.
FinDVer reports that do not fit in the context are cut: to their first
180,000 characters for Qwen3-8B, and to what fits in the 32,768-token
window for Qwen3-4B and 1.7B.

We use greedy decoding (temperature 0), so runs are deterministic and
differences come from the scaffold rather than from sampling. The one
exception is Qwen3-4B and 1.7B with thinking on FinDVer and CoverBench,
which use the
sampling settings Qwen3 recommends for thinking mode (temperature 0.6,
top-p 0.95, top-k 20, fixed seed). To check that greedy decoding does not
hurt thinking, we reran Qwen3-8B, zero-shot with thinking, on ContractNLI
three times with these settings: it reaches $0.692\pm0.007$ BA against
0.707 for greedy decoding on the same 899 claims, while generating about
100 more tokens per claim (Appendix~\ref{sec:decoding}).

\subsection{Token Cost and Evaluation}
\label{sec:metrics}

We call a scaffold with a thinking setting a \emph{condition}. The cost of
condition $S$ with model $M$ on one instance is
\begin{equation}
    T_M(S; c, D) = T_{\mathrm{in}} + T_{\mathrm{out}},
    \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
the input tokens (prompt, retrieved text and tool output) plus the
generated tokens (including reasoning), summed over all calls and counted
by the server; $\bar{T}_M(S)$ is its mean over a benchmark. We report the
two parts separately, because input tokens are cheaper than generated
ones: they are processed in parallel and can be reused through caching.
Since the labels are imbalanced (Table~\ref{tab:dataset_stats}), we report
balanced accuracy (BA, the mean recall over labels;
\citealp{brodersen2010balanced}) and macro-$F_1$. On ContractNLI, where we
have per-claim predictions for every run, we add 95\% bootstrap confidence
intervals \citep{efron1993bootstrap}, paired bootstrap tests with
Holm--Bonferroni correction \citep{holm1979}, and Cohen's $\kappa$
\citep{cohen1960kappa}.

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} gives BA for every model, scaffold and
benchmark. Three patterns hold across benchmarks. First, thinking raises
BA for every Qwen3 model, scaffold and benchmark, by 0.005 to 0.27.
Second, larger models are more accurate, but the gap is small on FinDVer:
the best 4B condition (0.782) is 0.044 below the best 8B condition.

on FinDVer (0.826) and CoT with thinking on CoverBench (0.678).

thinking adds $+0.047$ and $+0.176$ BA to CoT at 8B.

change. On all three benchmarks we also check when agentic retrieval finds

\noindent \textbf{Predictions depend on the gold evidence.} On ContractNLI,
removing the gold spans lowers accuracy from 0.699 to 0.464 and changes
28.3\% of predictions, while removing the same amount of other text leaves
accuracy at 0.702 and changes 5.7\%. On FinDVer, accuracy drops from 0.721
to 0.574 (52.1\% of predictions change), against 0.729 (5.9\%) for the
control. The drop comes from losing the evidence, not from a shorter
input. With only the gold evidence, accuracy rises to 0.793 and 0.744, so
the rest of the document partly distracts the model.

\noindent \textbf{Agentic retrieval often answers before it finds the
evidence.} It answers after 2.55 searches on ContractNLI and 2.02 on
FinDVer, having seen 55\% and 57\% of the gold evidence. On 25.6\% of
ContractNLI claims and 16.4\% of FinDVer claims it answers without having
retrieved any gold span, and 43\% and 65\% of these answers are still
scored correct. Its accuracy therefore overstates how often its answers
rest on evidence. On CoverBench, a third of the passages it retrieves are
repeats of earlier results.

\noindent \textbf{Chain-of-thought points to the right evidence but often
misquotes it.} Rationales share more words with the gold evidence than
with random passages (0.540 vs.\ 0.189 on ContractNLI, 0.251 vs.\ 0.089 on
FinDVer). However, only 68\%, 67\% and 48\% of their quotations appear word
for word in the document on ContractNLI, FinDVer and CoverBench.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether small LLMs should pay for the extra tokens that
inference-time scaffolds use in long-document claim verification. The
answer depends on the scaffold, the benchmark and the model size. At 8B,
native reasoning gives the largest gains, and CoT with thinking is the
most accurate condition on average. CoT adds nothing to native reasoning
on ContractNLI but does on CoverBench and FinDVer. Retrieval improves
accuracy on the long FinDVer reports and cuts input tokens on ContractNLI,
while the recursive language model reads the most tokens and is not the
most accurate on any benchmark. Below 8B, the cheapest good choice is CoT
without thinking on FinDVer and CoverBench and retrieval with thinking on
ContractNLI, and a 3B model without a thinking mode gains only from
retrieval. The model relies on the gold evidence when it has it, but
agentic retrieval often answers before finding it, and many
chain-of-thought quotes are not exact. We recommend reporting input and
generated tokens next to accuracy and choosing scaffolds per task and
model size.

\section*{Limitations}

\noindent \textbf{Models.}
We study one model family with native reasoning, Qwen3, from 1.7B to 8B,
and one model without it, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Results may differ for
other families or larger models. Some runs used an API and others a local
vLLM server, so numerical precision is not identical across runs; on
ContractNLI the two give zero-shot results within 0.002 BA.

\noindent \textbf{Confidence intervals.}
We report bootstrap confidence intervals and paired tests on ContractNLI,
where we have per-claim predictions for every run. For FinDVer and
CoverBench we report point estimates, so small differences there should
be read with care.

\noindent \textbf{Evidence annotations.}
The evidence-removal and evidence-recall probes need gold evidence, which
ContractNLI and FinDVer provide and CoverBench does not, so on CoverBench
we report only the stopping and quotation probes. On FinDVer the removal
probe starts from the gold paragraphs and their neighbours, because about
half of the reports exceed Qwen3-8B's 40,960-token context.

\noindent \textbf{Tokens as cost.}
We count tokens and do not measure latency or memory directly, and the
saving from caching input tokens depends on the serving setup. The
\textsc{rlm} statistics in Table~\ref{tab:rlm_behaviour} use one tokenizer
(tiktoken) for all benchmarks, while its cost $\bar{T}_M$ comes from the
server.

\noindent \textbf{Difficulty and evidence position.}
We do not break results down by number of reasoning hops or by where the
evidence appears in the document, so we cannot say whether a scaffold's
advantage holds for each kind of claim or whether retrieval reduces
lost-in-the-middle effects \citep{liu2024lost}.

\noindent \textbf{Decoding and output limits.}
We run each condition once. Most conditions use greedy decoding, but the
FinDVer and CoverBench runs of Qwen3-4B and 1.7B with thinking use
sampling, and our sampling check covers only one condition (Qwen3-8B,
zero-shot with thinking, ContractNLI). Generation is capped per call: on
FinDVer and CoverBench at 300 tokens without thinking (5 for \textsc{raw}
on FinDVer, 1,024 for \textsc{cot} on CoverBench) and 4,096 with thinking,
and on our local ContractNLI runs at
6,144 tokens, which greedy decoding reached on at most 0.1\% of calls. On
FinDVer and CoverBench, calls that return no answer, for example because
the prompt does not fit in the 32,768-token window of Qwen3-4B and 1.7B,
are left out of the score.

\noindent \textbf{Truncated documents.}
FinDVer reports longer than the context window are cut (\S\ref{sec:models}),
so for Qwen3-4B and 1.7B the full-document scaffolds see only the first
part of many reports.

\subsection{Prompts}
\label{sec:prompts}

Figures~\ref{fig:prompt_raw}--\ref{fig:prompt_cot} give the ContractNLI
prompts; \texttt{\{contract\}} and \texttt{\{hypothesis\}} are replaced by
the full contract and the claim (line breaks added for printing).
\textsc{raw} is written by hand. \typed{} and \textsc{cot} are generated by
DSPy from the same signature; \textsc{cot} only adds a \texttt{reasoning}
output field before \texttt{label}. Native reasoning uses the same prompts:
we switch it with the chat-template flag \texttt{enable\_thinking} (vLLM)
or the \texttt{reasoning} flag (OpenRouter), and the model then writes its
reasoning between \texttt{<think>} tags before answering. For \textsc{raw}
with thinking off we also add \texttt{/no\_think} at the end of the prompt.
Table~\ref{tab:reasoning_configs} shows what each combination of
\textsc{cot} and thinking produces.

Figures~\ref{fig:prompt_fv_raw}--\ref{fig:prompt_fv_agentic} give the
FinDVer prompts, with \texttt{\{document\}} and \texttt{\{statement\}}
replaced by the (possibly truncated) report and the claim. Unlike on
ContractNLI, the FinDVer \typed{} and \textsc{cot} prompts come from two
different DSPy signatures with slightly different instructions and label
words (\textit{entailed}/\textit{refuted} vs.\ \textit{TRUE}/\textit{FALSE}).
\textsc{rag} reuses the \textsc{raw} prompt with the retrieved paragraphs in
place of the report. For \textsc{raw} and \textsc{agentic rag} with
thinking off, \texttt{/no\_think} is added to the user messages.

Figures~\ref{fig:prompt_cb_rag} and~\ref{fig:prompt_cb_agentic} give the
CoverBench retrieval prompts. Unlike on the other two benchmarks, the
CoverBench \textsc{rag} prompt asks for step-by-step reasoning before the
verdict, and both prompts count claims that cannot be verified as
\textit{Incorrect}. Figures~\ref{fig:prompt_cb_raw}--\ref{fig:prompt_cb_cot}
give the CoverBench full-document prompts. \textsc{raw} is the zero-shot
prompt from the CoverBench paper \citep{jacovi2024coverbench}, \typed{}
uses the same instruction in a DSPy signature, and the \textsc{cot}
signature uses the instruction of the \textsc{rag} prompt, which asks for
step-by-step reasoning. Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct uses the same FinDVer and
CoverBench prompts for \textsc{raw}, \typed{} and \textsc{cot}.

\subsection{Decoding Check}
\label{sec:decoding}

We use greedy decoding for every condition. To check that this does not
change our conclusions, we reran Qwen3-8B with zero-shot prompting and
thinking on ContractNLI three times with the sampling settings Qwen3
recommends for thinking mode (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20).
Table~\ref{tab:greedy} compares the runs on the 899 claims that all three
finished. Sampling lowers BA by 0.015 on average, which is within the
confidence interval of the condition (Table~\ref{tab:cnli_ci}), and makes
the model generate about 100 more tokens per claim. Greedy decoding also
rarely got stuck: across all thinking-on ContractNLI runs, a reply reached
6{,}000 generated tokens in 0 of 5{,}185 calls at 8B, 6 at 4B and 2 at
1.7B.
